Whether you can legally resell a ticket in Europe depends on the country where the event takes place, not where you live. Ireland, Belgium and Italy ban or restrict resale above face value by law, and France punishes habitual resale without the organiser's permission. Germany and the Netherlands have no specific resale statute, so the organiser's ticket terms decide. In the UK, a cap on resale above the original price has been announced but is not yet law (as of November 2026). Everywhere in the EU, reselling tickets bought with bots is a banned commercial practice.

> **Key takeaways**
>
> - There is no single EU resale law. National rules range from a strict face-value cap (Ireland, Italy for large venues) to no specific statute (Germany, Netherlands).
> - Ireland: reselling above face value for designated events and venues with 1,000+ capacity can cost up to €100,000 or 2 years in prison.
> - UK: the government announced a ban on resale above the original price plus unavoidable fees in November 2025. As of November 2026 it is still a draft bill, not law.
> - EU-wide: reselling tickets that a trader bought with bots to get around purchase limits is blacklisted under the Unfair Commercial Practices Directive.
> - Personalised tickets are only safe to pass on through an official name change by the organiser.

This guide is not legal or tax advice.

## What counts as ticket resale and ticket touting?

**Ticket resale** is any sale of a ticket after the first sale by the organiser or its official ticket seller. That includes a friend buying your spare ticket, a fan-to-fan exchange and a secondary marketplace.

**Ticket touting** (called scalping in the US) means reselling tickets through unofficial channels for profit, usually above face value and often after buying in bulk. Most European laws target touting, not the fan who can no longer go.

**Face value** is the price the official seller charged for the ticket. Under the announced UK rules it will be defined as the original price plus unavoidable fees, including service charges. Other countries define it slightly differently, so check the local wording.

Three layers decide whether a resale is allowed:

1. **National law** in the country where the event takes place.
2. **The organiser's terms and conditions**, which you accepted when you bought the ticket. They can ban resale, cap the price or allow resale only through an official exchange.
3. **The rules of the platform** you use to sell, which may ban tickets that are personalised or not transferable.

### Ireland: the Sale of Tickets Act 2021

Ireland has the clearest face-value rule in Europe. A **designated event** is one with significant demand or where a ban on above-face-value resale is in the public interest, and a **designated venue** has a capacity of 1,000 or more. Venues and events are designated by the Department of Enterprise, Tourism and Employment, which keeps a register. Primary sellers must state on the ticket and in ads that it can't be resold above face value. Amateur sports clubs and registered charities are exempt for fundraising events. Secondary marketplaces may list such tickets only at face value or lower, and must show the original price and seat or standing area.

### UK: announced cap, not yet law

On 19 November 2025 the UK government announced plans to make resale above face value illegal for concerts, theatre, comedy, sport and other live events. The announced package also caps resale platform service fees, gives platforms a duty to monitor compliance and bans individuals from reselling more tickets than they were allowed to buy. The King's Speech on 13 May 2026 included a draft Ticket Tout Ban Bill for pre-legislative scrutiny, with no introduction date. On 4 November 2026 the House of Commons rejected a separate amendment for a sports-only cap. Until a bill passes, the existing rules apply: under Consumer Rights Act 2015 s. 90, resellers and platforms must tell buyers the face value, the seat or standing area and any restriction on who can use the ticket.

### Belgium, Italy and France in brief

- **Belgium** bans habitual resale outright and occasional resale above the original price. A buyer who paid too much can recover the excess. The Constitutional Court upheld the law in 2026 (judgment 77/2026).
- **Italy** relies on personalisation: tickets for shows in venues over 5,000 seats carry the holder's name and are checked against ID. Opera, classical and chamber music, jazz, ballet, dance and some other genres are exempt, and sport has its own rules.
- **France** targets the business of touting. The Conseil constitutionnel confirmed in 2018 that occasional private resale is not caught.

### EU-wide: the bot ban

Since 28 May 2022, Annex I No. 23a of the Unfair Commercial Practices Directive blacklists "reselling events tickets to consumers if the trader acquired them by using automated means to circumvent any limit imposed on the number of tickets that a person can buy". This applies in every EU country and targets traders, not private fans.

## Can I resell a ticket for more than I paid?

It depends on the event country and the ticket terms. In Ireland (designated events), Belgium and at large Italian venues, no. In France, a one-off private sale above face value is not a criminal offence, but habitual resale without permission is. In Germany and the Netherlands it is not illegal as such, but the organiser's terms may ban or cap a markup. A German appeal court (OLG Celle, 13 U 18/19) accepted a clause allowing private resale at up to face value plus 25 %.

Even where a markup is legal, it can backfire on your buyer. If the terms ban it, the organiser can cancel the ticket or refuse entry, and the buyer has paid extra for nothing.

**Example:** you bought a concert ticket for €59 in Germany and the terms allow private resale up to face value plus 25 %. The maximum is €59 × 1.25 = €73.75. The same ticket for a designated event in Dublin could legally go for no more than €59.

## What about personalised tickets?

A **personalised ticket** carries the attendee's name. If the venue checks names against ID, only the named person gets in, so selling the ticket without changing the name leaves your buyer outside. Citizens Information makes the same point for Ireland: a secondary seller's ticket may be cancelled, or entry allowed only for the original buyer's name.

Pass on a personalised ticket like this:

1. Read the terms: is a name change possible, until when and at what cost?
2. Ask the organiser to change the name to the new attendee.
3. Take payment only after the new name is confirmed.
4. Don't rely on a letter of authority or a copy of your ID. The organiser does not have to accept them.

There is also a refund catch. If the event is cancelled, the refund normally goes back to the original buyer's payment method, so agree in advance that you'll pass it on. Our guide on [event cancellation refunds in the EU](/en/guides/event-cancellation-refunds-eu) explains the rules.

### Checklist for sellers

- [ ] Checked the law in the country where the event takes place
- [ ] Read the organiser's terms on resale, price caps and approved platforms
- [ ] Looked for an official resale or exchange first
- [ ] Set a price no higher than the law and the terms allow
- [ ] Stated the face value, ticket type and any name on the ticket honestly
- [ ] Never posted the QR code or ticket PDF publicly, not even as "proof"
- [ ] Sent the ticket to one buyer only and kept no copies to pass on
- [ ] Used a payment method with buyer protection and kept the chat and receipt

## What can organisers do about resale?

If you run events, you can shape your resale market with your own terms and set-up, regardless of national law:

- **Write clear resale terms.** State whether private resale is allowed, at what maximum price and through which channels. German courts have accepted bans on selling to commercial traders (BGH, I ZR 74/06).
- **Use per-order limits.** A maximum number of tickets per order makes bulk buying harder, and the EU bot ban protects those limits against automated buying.
- **Personalise where it matters.** Names on tickets plus a name-change process make touting less attractive, at the cost of more admin at the door.
- **Offer a fair way out.** If fans who can't go have an official route, fewer tickets end up on secondary sites.
- **Follow local information duties.** In Ireland, primary sellers of designated events must print the face-value restriction on the ticket and in ads.

The German-language guide [Personalisierte Tickets](/ratgeber/personalisierte-tickets) goes deeper into personalisation from the organiser's side.

## Will the EU Digital Fairness Act change resale rules?

Possibly, but nothing has been decided. The European Commission announced its Digital Fairness Act proposal for late 2026, as part of a consumer package; as of early November 2026, no legal text had been published. The Commission's fitness check raised secondary ticketing, drip pricing and "from" prices under dynamic pricing, and consumer groups have called for a ban on above-face-value resale. Law firms expect any new rules to apply from the end of 2028 at the earliest. Until then, national laws and the UCPD bot ban remain the rules.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is it illegal to resell tickets in Europe?

Not in general. Private resale at or below face value is allowed in most countries, subject to the organiser's terms. Reselling above face value is illegal in Ireland for designated events and venues, in Belgium, and at large Italian venues outside the official resale, and habitual resale without permission is an offence in France.

### Is the UK ticket resale price cap law yet?

No. The government announced the cap in November 2025 and a draft Ticket Tout Ban Bill was in the King's Speech in May 2026, but as of November 2026 it has not been passed. Existing rules require resellers to disclose the face value, seat details and any restrictions.

### Can I resell a personalised ticket?

Only safely through an official name change or the organiser's resale. If names are checked at the door, a buyer with your ticket may be refused entry. Get the name changed before you take payment.

### What happens to my refund if a resold event is cancelled?

The refund normally goes to the original buyer and their payment method, not to the person who holds the ticket. If you sell privately, agree in writing that you'll pass on any refund.

### Which law applies if I live in Germany but the concert is in Dublin?

Ticket resale rules generally follow the country where the event takes place, so Irish face-value rules would apply to a designated Dublin event. The organiser's terms apply on top. If in doubt, check the national consumer authority's guidance.
