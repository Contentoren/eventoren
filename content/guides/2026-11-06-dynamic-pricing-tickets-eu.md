Dynamic ticket pricing means the price of the same ticket changes automatically during the sale according to demand. As of November 2026 it is not banned in the EU or the UK, but it is tightly bound by transparency rules: buyers must see the total price including every mandatory fee up front, and must not be misled about prices, scarcity or what a ticket includes. The EU's planned Digital Fairness Act could tighten these rules, but the Commission's proposal has not yet been published, and law firms expect it to apply from the end of 2028 at the earliest. For small and mid-sized organisers, a few fixed, pre-announced price tiers are almost always the better choice.

This guide is not legal or tax advice.

> **Key takeaways**
>
> - Dynamic pricing adjusts the price to live demand, usually by algorithm. Tiered pricing uses fixed prices and allocations announced in advance.
> - In the 2024 Oasis sale, the UK CMA found no evidence of algorithmic dynamic pricing. The problem was unannounced price tiers and misleading "platinum" labels.
> - EU law already requires total prices including mandatory fees (UCPD) and a notice when a price is personalised by automated decision-making (Consumer Rights Directive, as amended in 2019).
> - The Digital Fairness Act proposal was announced for late 2026; by early November 2026 no text had been published. It is expected to address drip pricing and unfair personalised pricing, with application from end-2028 at the earliest.
> - In the UK, the CMA can fine up to 10 % of turnover since April 2025, and its November 2025 guidance targets drip pricing directly.

## What is dynamic ticket pricing?

**Dynamic pricing** is a pricing method in which the seller continuously adjusts the price of the same product to current demand, typically through software. Prices rise when demand surges and may fall when it fades. Airlines and hotels made it familiar.

Tickets are a special case because every event is unique: one artist, one date, one venue. A fan who really wants to go cannot switch to a substitute, which is why consumer groups argue that dynamic pricing punishes enthusiasm.

It helps to separate the models that are often lumped together:

| Model                | How the price is set                     | Does it change during the sale?         | Example                                                   |
| -------------------- | ---------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- |
| Flat price           | once, in advance                         | no                                      | "Admission €15"                                           |
| Tiered pricing       | fixed tiers, each with an allocation     | yes, when a tier sells out              | 100 early bird at €12, then €15                           |
| Time-based phases    | fixed prices from set dates              | yes, on announced dates                 | €12 until 1 March, €15 after                              |
| Dynamic pricing      | seller or algorithm follows live demand  | yes, continuously, not fixed in advance | price rises while you queue                               |
| Personalised pricing | based on data about the individual buyer | differs per buyer                       | a different price depending on device or purchase history |

Tiers and phases have been standard practice for decades and are unproblematic when clearly announced. True demand pricing, and personalised pricing even more so, is under close regulatory scrutiny.

## What happened in the Oasis ticket sale?

The Oasis Live '25 sale in late summer 2024 shaped the European debate. Many fans waited for hours in online queues and then faced far higher prices than expected. Public opinion quickly blamed dynamic pricing.

The UK Competition and Markets Authority (CMA) opened an investigation into Ticketmaster on 5 September 2024. Its findings, published on 25 September 2025:

- **No algorithm found.** The CMA "has not found evidence" that prices were adjusted in real time by an algorithm.
- **Unannounced tiers.** Standing tickets were sold at two different prices. Fans in the queue were not told, and prices jumped as soon as the cheaper tickets sold out.
- **Misleading labels.** Some "platinum" tickets cost almost 2.5 times the price of standard tickets without sufficient explanation that they offered no extra benefit over some standard tickets in the same area.

Ticketmaster signed undertakings on 15 September 2025, voluntarily and without admitting wrongdoing. It must:

1. tell fans at least 24 hours in advance if tiered pricing is used for the same type of ticket;
2. show the range of prices when people join a queue and update them swiftly when cheaper tickets sell out;
3. not use misleading ticket labels;
4. report to the CMA regularly over two years.

The lesson for organisers of any size: the price change was not the problem. Missing information at the right moment was.

## Is dynamic pricing legal in the EU?

Yes. As of November 2026 no EU law bans demand-based ticket prices. Organisers may set and change prices freely, but existing consumer law applies to every price shown:

| Rule                                                                       | What it requires                                                                                                      | Effect on dynamic pricing                                                                                            |
| -------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| UCPD Art. 7(4)(c), Directive 2005/29/EC                                    | In an invitation to purchase, the price inclusive of taxes is material information                                    | Every displayed price must be the real total; fees can't be added later                                              |
| UCPD Art. 7(2)                                                             | Providing material information in an "unclear, unintelligible, ambiguous or untimely manner" is a misleading omission | A notice that prices may change must come before the purchase decision                                               |
| UCPD Art. 6(1)(d)                                                          | No deception about the price, how it is calculated, or a price advantage                                              | If you imply a fixed price, you may not quietly raise it                                                             |
| UCPD Annex I, No. 7                                                        | Falsely claiming an offer is available only for a very limited time is always unfair                                  | "Price rises in 5 minutes" must be true                                                                              |
| Consumer Rights Directive Art. 6(1)(ea), added by Directive (EU) 2019/2161 | Inform consumers when the price was personalised on the basis of automated decision-making                            | Applies to personalised prices. Recital 45 says plain dynamic or real-time pricing is not caught unless personalised |
| UCPD Annex I, No. 23a                                                      | Reselling tickets bought with bots that bypass purchase limits is always unfair                                       | Relevant to resale, not to primary pricing                                                                           |

National law sits on top. Germany's Price Indication Ordinance requires a total price (§ 3 PAngV). Belgium's 2013 resale law requires primary sellers to state the final price on the ticket. Italy requires nominative tickets for shows in venues above 5,000 capacity. Ireland bans resale above face value for designated events and venues of 1,000+ capacity.

### Drip pricing is already unlawful

**Drip pricing** means advertising a low price and adding unavoidable fees step by step during checkout. Under the UCPD, that hides material information and is a misleading omission. Combined with dynamic prices it becomes especially risky, because the buyer cannot compare the final price with anything. The Digital Fairness Act is expected to tighten this further.

## What will the Digital Fairness Act change?

The **Digital Fairness Act (DFA)** is a planned EU law to update consumer protection for online business, based on the Commission's Fitness Check of EU consumer law. As of 7 November 2026:

- **No legal text yet.** The Commission announced its proposal for late 2026, as part of a consumer package with the revision of the Consumer Protection Cooperation Regulation. As of early November 2026, no legal text had been published.
- **Expected topics:** dark patterns, addictive design, subscription traps, unfair personalisation and pricing, influencer marketing. Ticketing angles flagged in the Fitness Check and by MEPs include drip pricing, misleading "from" prices under dynamic pricing, and secondary ticketing.
- **Political pressure:** the S&D group in the European Parliament called for a ban on dynamic and personalised pricing for live events on 22 October 2026. Euroconsumers has called for a ban on above-face-value resale. These are political demands, not law.
- **Timing:** after the proposal, Parliament and Council negotiate. Law firms expect the DFA to apply from the end of 2028 at the earliest.

What ends up in the final text is open. Any new pricing model you build now should survive stricter transparency rules. We will update this guide once the proposal is published.

## What are the rules in the UK?

The UK is outside EU law but has moved furthest on enforcement:

| Development                                         | Status (as of November 2026)                                                                                                                                                                           |
| --------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024 | Since April 2025 the CMA can decide itself that consumer law was broken and fine firms up to 10 % of turnover, without going to court                                                                  |
| CMA price transparency guidance (CMA209)            | Published 18 November 2025, covers mandatory fees, drip pricing and partitioned pricing                                                                                                                |
| Ticketmaster undertakings                           | Accepted 25 September 2025, two years of reporting                                                                                                                                                     |
| Resale price cap                                    | Announced 19 November 2025: resale above original price plus unavoidable fees to become illegal, resale fees capped. Draft Ticket Tout Ban Bill named in the King's Speech of 13 May 2026; not yet law |
| Dynamic pricing in primary sales                    | The government ran a call for evidence and welcomed a commitment by the Society of Ticket Agents and Retailers (STAR) to develop best practice on price transparency                                   |
| Sports-only resale cap amendment                    | Rejected by the Commons on 4 November 2026 (286–62)                                                                                                                                                    |

If you sell to UK fans, the CMA's 24-hour tier notice from the Oasis case is the practical benchmark, even though it formally binds only Ticketmaster.

## Why dynamic pricing rarely fits small organisers

Major tours with hundreds of thousands of fans have demand data, pricing teams and specialist tools. For a 300-capacity club, a sports association or a school ball committee, the maths is different:

- **Too little data.** With a few hundred tickets, demand signals are noise. An algorithm reacts to chance.
- **Trust is your capital.** Your audience often knows you personally. Someone who learns a friend paid €8 less for the same ticket may not come back.
- **Legal risk without a legal team.** Every price change must be displayed correctly. Errors in total prices or scarcity claims can trigger complaints and enforcement.
- **It invites waiting.** If buyers expect prices might fall, they hold off. In presale, that is the opposite of what you want.

## What are fair alternatives to dynamic pricing?

You can capture most of the benefit of staged prices without the downsides. The principle: every price is fixed and public before sales start.

### Model 1: tiers by allocation

| Tier       | Allocation         | Price | What buyers see up front                 |
| ---------- | ------------------ | ----- | ---------------------------------------- |
| Early bird | 80                 | €12   | "80 early bird tickets at €12, then €15" |
| Standard   | 180                | €15   | price plus the door price                |
| Door       | remaining capacity | €18   | "€18 at the door while capacity lasts"   |

Worked example for a 260-capacity hall. Buyers know every price and what triggers the change from the start, which is exactly the transparency the CMA required in the Oasis case. More on sizing in [early bird tickets](/en/guides/early-bird-tickets), and on the maths in [how to price event tickets](/en/guides/how-to-price-event-tickets).

### Model 2: phases by date

Switch on fixed, announced dates instead: "€12 until 31 January, €15 from 1 February." Buyers can plan; you must make the switch exactly on time.

### Checklist: transparent price tiers

- [ ] Fix all tiers, prices and allocations before sales start
- [ ] Name all tiers on the event page and in ads, not only the cheapest
- [ ] Show every price as a total including all mandatory fees
- [ ] Use "from" prices only while that tier is actually available
- [ ] Make scarcity claims ("20 early bird left") only when true
- [ ] Name tiers after what they include; a "premium" ticket needs a real extra
- [ ] Publish the door price from day one
- [ ] Never drop back to a lower price without announcing it first

## What do we recommend?

Our recommendation for events up to a few thousand guests, as of November 2026:

1. **Skip true dynamic pricing.** The possible extra revenue is not worth the loss of trust and the legal exposure.
2. **Use two or three fixed tiers.** Early bird, standard and door cover most events.
3. **Explain the rules in one sentence.** "The first 80 tickets cost €12, then €15, and €18 at the door."
4. **Always show the total price.** Fees belong in the advertised price, not the last checkout step. See our [fee comparison](/en/guides/ticketing-platforms-fees-compared) for what platforms charge.
5. **Never raise prices silently.** If costs force a change, announce the new tier before it applies.
6. **Watch the DFA.** The proposal will show whether "from" prices and demand pricing for live events face new limits.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is dynamic ticket pricing legal in Europe?

Yes, as of November 2026 neither the EU nor the UK bans it. Sellers must show the total price including mandatory fees, must not mislead about price, scarcity or how the price is calculated, and must flag prices personalised by automated decision-making. National rules may add requirements.

### What is the difference between dynamic and tiered pricing?

Dynamic pricing changes the price continuously according to demand, often by algorithm. Tiered pricing uses fixed tiers and allocations set before the sale, and the price only moves when a tier sells out. According to the CMA, the 2024 Oasis sale used tiered pricing, not algorithmic dynamic pricing.

### Will the Digital Fairness Act ban dynamic pricing?

Nobody knows yet. The proposal was announced for late 2026, with no text published by early November. Some MEPs want a ban for live events, but the Commission has not committed to one. Law firms expect the DFA to apply from the end of 2028 at the earliest.

### What is drip pricing?

Drip pricing means advertising a low price and adding unavoidable fees step by step during checkout. In the EU it is already a misleading omission under the Unfair Commercial Practices Directive. In the UK, the CMA's November 2025 price transparency guidance addresses it directly.

### Can I raise ticket prices during the presale?

Yes, if you do it transparently. Announce the tiers in advance, for example "the first 80 tickets at €12, then €15". Tickets already sold keep their price, and any scarcity or deadline claims must be accurate.
