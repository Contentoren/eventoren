A box office is where guests buy tickets on the day, at the venue. You can run it with cash, with a card terminal, or fully cashless with guests paying on their own phones via a QR code. The choice matters for more than speed: in several European countries, the moment you record sales on an electronic till, fiscal rules kick in, such as certified security devices in Germany and Austria, certified till software in France and fiscal ticketing systems in Italy. This guide compares the options, explains the national rules in plain language, and gives you pricing advice, a staffing plan and a day-of checklist, as of November 2026.

> **Key takeaways**
>
> - Three models: cash box, electronic till or card terminal, and self-service where the guest buys online on their own phone at the door.
> - Cash-register rules are national. Germany requires a certified security device (TSE) for electronic tills that can take cash; Austria has a register obligation above turnover thresholds; France regulates till software; Italy has its own fiscal ticketing system for shows.
> - A purely online, cashless sale generally falls outside till rules in Germany and Austria, but get this confirmed for your setup and country.
> - Door prices are usually higher than presale prices. Show the total price including all mandatory fees.
> - Keep the box office physically separate from ticket scanning, or buyers will block guests who already have tickets.

This guide is not legal or tax advice.

## What is a box office?

A **box office** (also called the door, walk-up sales or the ticket desk) is the point of sale where guests buy admission on the day of the event. It complements advance sales for spontaneous visitors and remaining capacity. For club nights, small concerts, theatre and community events, door sales can be a meaningful share of revenue.

There are three basic models:

| Model                            | How it works                                                                  | Typical for                                 |
| -------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------- |
| Cash box                         | Cash tin, float, paper record at the end                                      | Village fêtes, club fundraisers, small gigs |
| Electronic till or card terminal | Till app or register, card reader, often also cash                            | Theatres, clubs, regular promoters          |
| Self-service via QR code         | Guest scans a poster, buys online on their phone, receives a regular e-ticket | Club nights, festivals, younger audiences   |

## Cash or cashless: which suits your event?

| Criterion       | Cash                                      | Card terminal                     | Self-service QR                               |
| --------------- | ----------------------------------------- | --------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------- |
| Hardware        | Cash tin, float                           | Terminal or phone with tap-to-pay | None, just a poster                           |
| Staff           | Cashier, ideally two people               | Cashier                           | No cashier, one helper for questions          |
| Speed per guest | Slow with change                          | Medium                            | Guests buy in parallel while queuing          |
| Internet needed | No                                        | Usually                           | Yes, on the guest's phone                     |
| Main risk       | Theft, counting errors, counterfeit notes | Terminal failure                  | Guest without phone, card or signal           |
| Fiscal duties   | Paper cash records                        | Depends on country and system     | Treated like an online sale in many countries |
| Ticket at entry | Wristband, stamp or paper ticket          | Depends on the system             | Regular QR ticket, scanned like any other     |

**Rule of thumb:** the younger and more digital your audience and the bigger the rush, the stronger the case for cashless. For older audiences or village events, plan a person who helps guests without a smartphone, or keep a cash option.

### May you refuse cash?

Euro banknotes are legal tender, but how far a private organiser may exclude cash depends on national law. In Germany, the guidance of the German skilled-crafts association (ZDH) states that the obligation to accept cash can be excluded under private law if you announce it clearly in advance, for instance in your terms or by a notice. Wherever you are, put "box office: cashless only" on the event page, in the info email and at the entrance, and check your national rules.

## Which cash-register rules apply in your country?

Rules differ widely. The table gives examples from four countries as of November 2026. It is a starting point, not a complete list.

| Country | Core rule                                                                                                                                                                                                          | Card payments count?                                      | Pure online sale?                                                                                                     |
| ------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Germany | No general obligation to use a till; an open cash box with records is allowed. Electronic tills must have a certified security device (TSE), issue receipts and be registered with the tax office within one month | The TSE duty is triggered if the system _can_ record cash | A system that allows no cash payments on site needs no TSE, per the finance ministry FAQ quoted by ZDH                |
| Austria | Electronic register required from €15,000 net annual turnover per business if cash takings exceed €7,500 net; receipts for every cash payment; fines up to €5,000                                                  | Yes, card payments count as cash transactions             | Online-shop sales are exempt from the register duty if customers don't pay in cash, but receipts must still be issued |
| France  | No duty to own a till, but VAT-registered businesses that record consumer payments in till software must use software certified or attested as tamper-proof (art. 286 I 3° bis CGI)                                | Yes, all payment methods are covered                      | Small businesses under the VAT franchise are excluded; check with your adviser                                        |
| Italy   | For shows and entertainment, admission tickets must be issued with approved fiscal meters or automated ticketing systems, managed by SIAE for the tax agency (D.M. 13 July 2000)                                   | Yes                                                       | Check with an Italian adviser whether your online platform can issue compliant tickets                                |

### Germany in more detail

- **Open cash box allowed.** With a cash tin and a paper cash report, there's no TSE and no receipt duty.
- **Electronic till = TSE.** § 146a of the Fiscal Code and the KassenSichV regulation require a certified technical security device for electronic recording systems, including tablet and phone till apps.
- **"Can" is enough.** According to the ministry's application decree, a system has a till function if it _can_ process at least partly cash payments, including vouchers or drink tokens accepted on site. An unused cash button still counts.
- **Registration.** Since 1 July 2025, new tills must be reported via ELSTER within one month of acquisition, including rented ones, and deregistered within one month of removal.
- **Fines.** Up to €25,000 for using an unprotected electronic till.

### Austria in more detail

Austria's register obligation depends on two thresholds that both must be exceeded. Card payments count as cash here, so a card terminal alone doesn't get you out of the rules. Small club festivities of non-profit associations (the classic fire-brigade party) have their own exemptions under strict conditions. Online-shop sales without direct cash payment are exempt from the register duty, but you still need receipts.

### Everywhere else

Many countries have fiscal-till or receipt rules, and some cover card payments too. Before your first door sale abroad, ask a local tax adviser three questions: Do I need a certified till? Do card payments count? Is a guest buying online on their own phone treated as an online sale?

## How should you price tickets at the door?

Door prices are usually **higher than presale prices**. That rewards early buyers, covers the extra effort and reduces uncertainty about numbers. Announce both from day one ("Presale €15, door €18"), which nudges people to buy early. How to set tiers in general is covered in [how to price event tickets](/en/guides/how-to-price-event-tickets).

Things to get right:

- **Total price.** The door price must include every mandatory fee. EU consumer law treats fees revealed late as misleading.
- **VAT.** Door sales carry the same VAT rate as presale tickets for the same event. Rates by country are in [VAT on event tickets in Europe](/en/guides/vat-on-event-tickets-europe).
- **Round numbers for cash.** €15 or €20 saves coins; €17.50 slows every sale.
- **A door allocation.** Decide how many tickets you hold back for the door. Weezevent notes that some organisers keep a small door quota even for sold-out shows, to reduce frustration and black-market resale.

**Worked example (fees):** on Eventoren, an €18 door ticket costs 3.9 % × €18 + €0.49 = €1.19 in fees, including payment processing and VAT; the €15 presale ticket costs €1.08 (as of November 2026).

### How much float do you need for cash?

**Example calculation:** door price €15, around 80 sales expected, many guests paying with €20 and €50 notes. A €20 note needs €5 change; a €50 needs €35. A float of 10 × €5, 10 × €10 and 5 × €20 (€250) covers the first dozen change cases until incoming notes recirculate. These quantities are an assumption; adjust them to your price and audience.

## How many people do you need at the box office?

**Example staffing plan** for an evening with an expected 100 door sales. Role counts are assumptions for orientation.

| Role                                     | Cash box                | Card terminal | Self-service QR |
| ---------------------------------------- | ----------------------- | ------------- | --------------- |
| Cashier                                  | 2 (four-eyes principle) | 1 to 2        | 0               |
| Helper explaining the poster and payment | 0                       | 0             | 1               |
| Runner for change and cash drops         | 1                       | 0             | 0               |
| Count at closing                         | 2, together             | 1 (export)    | 1 (export)      |
| **Total during peak**                    | **3**                   | **1 to 2**    | **1**           |

For cash, add these safety rules:

- Never one person alone at the cash tin; always count in pairs.
- Skim large notes regularly into a safe, with a signed withdrawal slip.
- Keep the cash tin behind the table, out of guests' reach.
- Check €50 notes and above: feel, look, tilt.
- Don't carry the takings home alone.

**Example cash report:** opening float €200; one €500 drop to the safe during the evening; closing count €438. Takings = €438 − €200 + €500 = €738. Ticket stubs show 41 sales × €18 = €738. The cash balances.

## How does the box office run on the day?

**Before the event**

- [ ] Door allocation and door price set and published
- [ ] Payment route chosen: cash, card, self-service or a mix
- [ ] For tills: fiscal requirements of your country checked, device registered where required
- [ ] For cash: float from the bank, cash report template printed
- [ ] For cashless: signal at the box-office spot tested on two networks

**On the day**

- [ ] Box office set up **away from the scan lanes**
- [ ] Visible sign with price and accepted payment methods
- [ ] "Sold out" sign ready and hung early when needed
- [ ] Bought tickets scanned at entry like any other ticket

**After the event**

- [ ] Cash counted in pairs, or sales export downloaded
- [ ] Cash report or export filed with your accounts
- [ ] Rented tills deregistered where required

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I need a cash register for door sales?

It depends on your country. Germany has no general till obligation: an open cash box with proper records is allowed, but electronic tills that can take cash need a certified TSE. Austria requires a register above €15,000 turnover with more than €7,500 in cash and card takings. Check your national rules before the event.

### Does a card terminal avoid cash-register rules?

Not everywhere. In Austria, card payments count as cash transactions for the register obligation, and France's till-software rules cover all payment methods. In Germany, the TSE duty depends on whether the system can record cash at all.

### Is a self-service QR box office legal?

Selling tickets online to a guest who uses their own phone is an ordinary online sale. German and Austrian guidance treats systems without on-site cash payment differently from tills, but rules vary, so confirm your setup with a tax adviser. Make sure you announce in advance that the door is cashless.

### Can the door price be higher than the presale price?

Yes, that's common and allowed. Show the door price as a total price including all mandatory fees, and communicate both prices early to encourage presales.

### What if a guest has no smartphone at a cashless door?

Decide in advance. Options include a helper's device for a supervised purchase, a guest-list entry for genuine exceptions, or turning the guest away with a clear, polite explanation that matches what you announced.
