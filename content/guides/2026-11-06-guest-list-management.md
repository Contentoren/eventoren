A guest list is the list of people admitted by name rather than with a purchased ticket: artists, crew, press, sponsors, helpers, the organiser's friends and promoters' guests. To work on the night it needs five columns (name, plus-ones, category, invited by, check-in), clear plus-one rules and **a fixed quota for everyone allowed to invite**. Without those, the list grows unchecked, capacity runs short and the door turns into a debate. Below you get a copy-ready template, rules for plus-ones and promoter quotas, the door process, GDPR basics and a way to plan no-shows, as of November 2026.

> **Key takeaways**
>
> - One row per main guest, plus-ones as a number. Every row answers: who invited them, which category, how many may come along?
> - Fixed quotas per promoter, DJ or committee member stop the list from eating your capacity.
> - Set plus-one rules in advance: maximum number, whether names are required, and whether the plus-one may arrive alone.
> - Guest-list guests count towards capacity. Plan no-shows with your own data, never by overbooking past the venue limit.
> - A guest list is personal data under the GDPR: collect only what you need, keep it out of sight and delete it after the event.

## What is a guest list?

A **guest list** is a record of invited people who are admitted at the door by name, or with a free ticket, instead of a purchased ticket. Places are usually free, sometimes discounted ("reduced entry before midnight"), and often come with plus-ones.

Typical groups:

| Category              | Who                                      | Typical allowance                                          |
| --------------------- | ---------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- |
| Artists and crew      | Band, DJ, technicians and their partners | Set in the performance contract, often +1 or +2 per artist |
| Press and media       | Journalists, photographers, creators     | On request, usually +0 or +1                               |
| Sponsors and partners | Contacts and their clients               | Set in the sponsorship agreement                           |
| Promoters             | Guests a promoter brings                 | Fixed quota per promoter                                   |
| Team and helpers      | Off-duty volunteers                      | By agreement                                               |
| Friends and family    | Organiser's personal guests              | Keep small                                                 |
| Competition winners   | Social media or radio giveaways          | Exactly as advertised                                      |

At a club night the list is a marketing tool; at a school concert or charity gala it's a thank-you to helpers and sponsors. How guest lists and promoters fit together in nightlife is covered in [how to run a club night](/en/guides/how-to-run-a-club-night).

## Guest list template: which columns do you need?

Copy this template into Excel, LibreOffice or Google Sheets. All example entries are fictitious.

| Name          | Plus-ones | Category       | Invited by   | Check-in |
| ------------- | --------- | -------------- | ------------ | -------- |
| Lena Hoffmann | 1         | Press          | Organiser    |          |
| Murat Yilmaz  | 2         | Artist         | Booking      |          |
| Aoife Byrne   | 0         | Sponsor        | Committee    |          |
| Tom de Vries  | 1         | Promoter guest | Promoter Ali |          |
| Sophie Martin | 1         | Promoter guest | Promoter Ali |          |
| Ben Schulz    | 0         | Helper         | Rota         |          |
| Clara Rossi   | 1         | Competition    | Social media |          |

How to use each column:

- **Name:** first and last name as on their ID. Nicknames or "Ali + 3" cause arguments at the door.
- **Plus-ones:** number of people admitted in addition to the main guest. 0 means the guest alone.
- **Category:** fixed values from a drop-down (press, artist, sponsor, promoter guest, helper, friends, competition), so you can evaluate later.
- **Invited by:** who added the guest. The most important column for quotas and for questions at the door.
- **Check-in:** ticked at the door with time and plus-ones actually admitted, for example "21:40, 1/1".

### Optional columns

- **Note:** "backstage access", "arrives after midnight", "wheelchair user".
- **Area or wristband:** if guests may enter different zones.
- **Contact:** email or phone only if you really need it, for example to send a free ticket.

Leave out date of birth, address or ID numbers unless the event genuinely requires them. Less data means less risk. If there's an age limit, check ID at the door; it doesn't need to go on the list.

## How should you handle plus-ones?

Plus-ones cause most door disputes. Fix the rules before the event and give them to everyone who may invite:

| Rule                    | Recommendation                                                | Why                                                 |
| ----------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------- |
| Maximum per guest       | 0 to 2; exceptions only via the event lead                    | Otherwise the list grows unchecked                  |
| Plus-one name required? | Optional for press and sponsors, sensible for promoter guests | Known names mean less abuse                         |
| Arrive together         | Plus-ones only with the main guest                            | Stops a "+1" turning up alone and claiming the name |
| Late additions          | Only until a cut-off, e.g. 18:00 on the day                   | Gives the door a fixed list                         |
| Unused places           | Lapse, not transferable                                       | No "my friend is coming later instead of me"        |

**Wording for the invitation:** "You're on the guest list +1. Please arrive together and bring ID. The guest list closes at 23:00."

## How do you set promoter quotas?

A **guest list quota** is the maximum number of places, including plus-ones, that one person or group may hand out. Quotas are the single most effective tool for keeping a list plannable.

### Worked example: quotas for a 400-capacity club night

You decide to reserve at most 10 % of capacity for the guest list, so 40 places:

| Quota                   | Places incl. plus-ones | Rule                |
| ----------------------- | ---------------------- | ------------------- |
| DJs (2 artists)         | 6                      | +2 each             |
| Promoter Ali            | 10                     | Guests before 23:00 |
| Promoter Mia            | 10                     | Guests before 23:00 |
| Press and photographers | 4                      | +0 or +1            |
| Organiser and team      | 6                      | By agreement        |
| Door manager reserve    | 4                      | Emergencies only    |
| **Total**               | **40**                 | 10 % of capacity    |

The 10 % is an assumption for this example. A charity gala with many sponsors may need more; a sold-out concert less. What matters is that you fix it in advance and that **all quotas plus tickets sold never exceed capacity**.

### Rules for promoters

- **A hard cap** per promoter, including plus-ones.
- **A cut-off** for additions before doors open.
- **A time limit:** the list is valid until a set time; after that, regular price or box office.
- **Evaluation:** after each event, count per promoter how many guests actually came. Promoters with full lists and empty doors get smaller quotas.

If you want to reward promoters for selling rather than for listing, a separate ticket type or [presale codes](/en/guides/presale-codes) are often cleaner.

## How many guest-list guests actually turn up?

Not everyone on the list comes. With free invitations, the threshold for signing up and staying home is low. There is no universal no-show rate; it depends on event type, weather, time and whether the guest paid anything.

How to deal with it:

- **Measure your own numbers.** After every event, count per category how many listed guests and plus-ones came. After two or three events you have a reliable rate.
- **Don't overbook blindly.** Guest-list guests count towards capacity. If everyone comes for once, the venue still must not be overfull. The licensed capacity is a hard limit.
- **Reserve, don't overbook.** Keep a small reserve quota for the door manager instead of filling the list beyond capacity.
- **A reminder on the day.** A personal message ("You're on the list +1 tonight, until 23:00") increases commitment.
- **Free tickets instead of names.** A ticket with a QR code feels more concrete and speeds up entry, because the guest is scanned rather than searched for. Whether it improves turnout, your own figures will show.

**Worked example:** your last three parties had 45, 50 and 40 guest-list places; 30, 36 and 28 people came. That's 94 of 135 places, about 70 %. Plan the bar and staffing for 70 %, but only sell as many tickets as still fit if 100 % of the list turns up.

## How does guest-list entry work at the door?

The guest list needs **its own lane, or at least its own person**. Searching for a name takes longer than a scan and otherwise holds up the main lanes; the maths is in [event entry queue management](/en/guides/event-entry-queue-management).

**Door process:**

1. Guest gives first and last name and, if needed, who invited them.
2. Helper finds the name on the list or in the system.
3. ID check if the list is name-checked or there's an age limit.
4. Plus-ones matched to the number on the list. More people than listed: the rest pay or go to the box office.
5. Check-in marked, with plus-ones actually admitted.
6. Wristband or stamp, if you use them.

### What if something goes wrong at the door?

| Situation                        | Response                                                                        |
| -------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Name not on the list             | Decline politely or refer to the door manager. Never improvise "that's fine".   |
| Guest says a promoter added them | Call the promoter. If unreachable: no entry or regular price.                   |
| More plus-ones than allowed      | Admit the listed number; the rest pay.                                          |
| Name already checked in          | Treat like a duplicate scan: door manager, name the time of the first check-in. |
| After the list's time limit      | As announced: regular price or box office.                                      |

**Key rule:** one person decides exceptions, the door manager. If every helper makes their own exceptions, the list is worthless after an hour.

### Guest list checklist before the event

- [ ] Total guest-list allowance set, including plus-ones
- [ ] Quotas split between promoters, artists, press, sponsors and team
- [ ] Plus-one rules, time limit and cut-off communicated
- [ ] List closed at the cut-off and sorted alphabetically
- [ ] Separate lane or person for the guest list planned
- [ ] Door manager named as the only authority for exceptions
- [ ] After the event: turnout per category and promoter evaluated, list deleted

## What does the GDPR mean for guest lists?

A guest list contains **personal data** under the GDPR: at least names, often contact details. That leads to a few straightforward rules:

- **Lawful basis.** For entry control, organisers usually rely on legitimate interest (Art. 6(1)(f)); for free tickets sent by email, on performing the invitation or contract (Art. 6(1)(b)).
- **Data minimisation.** Collect only fields you need for entry (Art. 5(1)(c)).
- **Restrict access.** Don't leave printed lists lying around, collect and shred them afterwards, and don't share digital lists in group chats with every helper.
- **Delete.** Delete guest lists and scan logs once their purpose is fulfilled, unless you need them for tax records.
- **No reuse without a basis.** Don't pass the list to sponsors or add it to your newsletter. Marketing email generally needs consent, or the narrow existing-customer exception under ePrivacy rules.
- **Transparency.** Explain in the invitation or privacy notice what the data is used for.

More on lawful bases, photos and newsletters is in our [GDPR guide for event organisers](/en/guides/gdpr-for-event-organisers).

## Frequently asked questions

### What does "+1" mean on a guest list?

"+1" means the listed guest may bring one companion who is also admitted without a ticket. "+2" means two companions. Usually the companion is only admitted together with the main guest.

### What share of capacity should go on the guest list?

There's no fixed rule. Many organisers keep it small so enough tickets remain for sale. Set the number before the event and make sure tickets sold plus all guest-list places, including plus-ones, never exceed the licensed capacity.

### Is there a free guest list template?

Yes: the five-column table in this guide (name, plus-ones, category, invited by, check-in) works in any spreadsheet. Add optional columns for notes, area and contact details only when you need them.

### Can I use the guest list for my newsletter after the event?

Not without more. Marketing emails generally need consent under EU rules, and the guest list was collected for entry. Delete it after the event unless you have another lawful reason to keep it.

### Paper or digital guest list?

Paper works for small events with one entrance. As soon as several people maintain the list, several entrances check guests in, or additions arrive close to doors, a digital list with live check-in is more reliable. Keep a printout as a backup anyway.
