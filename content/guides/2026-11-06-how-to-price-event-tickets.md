To price event tickets, add up every fixed cost of the event, work out what is left of each ticket after VAT, the ticketing fee and other per-ticket costs, and divide the fixed costs by that amount. The result is your break-even: the number of tickets you must sell before you stop losing money. If that number sits above roughly 75–80 % of your capacity, the price is too low or the event is too expensive. Below, we work through a complete example for a 300-capacity concert in euros, including VAT, a 3.9 % + €0.49 ticketing fee and three price tiers (as of November 2026).

> **Key takeaways**
>
> - Break-even tickets = fixed costs ÷ contribution per ticket. Contribution is the gross price minus VAT, the ticketing fee and any other per-ticket cost.
> - Plan to cover your costs at 70–80 % of capacity. A sell-out is the bonus, not the plan.
> - In our worked example (300 capacity, €4,785 fixed costs, 9 % VAT), €22 tickets break even at 255 sales and €26 tickets at 215.
> - Passing a 3.9 % + €0.49 fee on to buyers lowers that break-even from 255 to 238 tickets, but the buyer sees €23.35 instead of €22.
> - Tiers (early bird, standard, door) bring money and certainty forward. They do not fix a broken budget.

## What costs go into a ticket price?

Every pricing decision starts with an honest cost list split into two groups:

- **Fixed costs** stay the same whether 50 or 300 people come: venue hire, artist fee, sound and light, marketing, insurance, core staff.
- **Variable costs** rise with each ticket sold: VAT included in the ticket price, the ticketing fee, a percentage-based music licence in some countries, wristbands, an included drink.

| Cost line          | Fixed or variable? | Typical items                                          | What to check (as of November 2026)                         |
| ------------------ | ------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------- |
| Venue              | fixed              | hire, cleaning, utilities                              | a bar guarantee or revenue share turns part of it variable  |
| Artists            | fixed              | fee, travel, hotel, rider                              | a door deal (percentage of ticket income) makes it variable |
| Production         | fixed              | PA, lights, technician, backline                       |                                                             |
| Staff and security | fixed or stepped   | door staff, cloakroom, stewards                        | minimum wages differ by country                             |
| Marketing          | fixed              | posters, social ads, print                             |                                                             |
| Insurance          | fixed              | public liability                                       |                                                             |
| Music licensing    | depends            | your national collecting society, e.g. GEMA in Germany | some tariffs are a percentage of ticket income, others flat |
| Ticketing          | variable           | fee per paid ticket                                    | compare including payment processing and VAT                |
| VAT                | variable           | part of the gross ticket price                         | rate depends on the event type and country                  |

The two classic mistakes: forgetting that VAT sits inside the price your guest pays, and having no buffer. Add 10 % of your fixed costs as contingency. For a full cost template, see our [event budget template](/en/guides/event-budget-template).

## What is the break-even formula for event tickets?

**Break-even** is the number of tickets at which income exactly covers costs. The formula:

**Break-even tickets = fixed costs ÷ contribution per ticket**

**Contribution per ticket** is what one ticket leaves after all variable costs:

**Contribution = gross price − VAT − ticketing fee − other per-ticket costs**

You can also run it backwards. Decide on a realistic attendance, say 75 % of capacity, divide your fixed costs by that number to get the contribution you need, then add VAT and per-ticket costs on top. That is your minimum price, before you look at the market.

## Worked example: a 300-capacity concert in Utrecht

A cultural association runs a concert by a regional band in a 300-capacity standing venue in Utrecht in spring 2027. It is VAT-registered, and Dutch admission to music performances carries a 9 % reduced rate. All figures are a **worked example**; your numbers will differ.

### Step 1: Fixed costs

| Item                                   | Amount        |
| -------------------------------------- | ------------- |
| Venue hire incl. cleaning              | €900.00       |
| Band fee (flat)                        | €1,200.00     |
| Sound and light incl. technician       | €600.00       |
| Door and cloakroom staff               | €360.00       |
| Security contractor                    | €400.00       |
| Marketing: posters, flyers, social ads | €350.00       |
| Public liability insurance             | €120.00       |
| Band catering and travel               | €250.00       |
| Wristbands, licences, sundries         | €170.00       |
| **Subtotal**                           | **€4,350.00** |
| Contingency 10 %                       | €435.00       |
| **Total fixed costs**                  | **€4,785.00** |

### Step 2: Contribution per ticket at €22

| Item                                                     | Per ticket |
| -------------------------------------------------------- | ---------- |
| Gross ticket price                                       | €22.00     |
| − VAT 9 % (22 − 22 ÷ 1.09)                               | − €1.82    |
| − Ticketing fee: 3.9 % × €22 + €0.49 (organiser absorbs) | − €1.35    |
| **Contribution**                                         | **€18.83** |

If your music licence is a percentage of ticket income, deduct it here as well. We left it in the fixed costs to keep the example portable across countries.

### Step 3: Break-even

€4,785 ÷ €18.83 = 254.1, so **255 tickets**, or 85 % of capacity. That is too tight: one rainy evening or a competing event and the association loses money. It has three levers: cut costs, raise the price, or pass the fee on to buyers.

## How does the price change the break-even?

A **sensitivity table** shows how a decision variable changes the outcome. Here is the break-even at different prices, with all other figures unchanged (worked example).

| Gross price | VAT (9 %) | Fee   | Contribution | Break-even | Capacity needed    |
| ----------- | --------- | ----- | ------------ | ---------- | ------------------ |
| €16         | €1.32     | €1.11 | €13.57       | 353        | 118 % (impossible) |
| €18         | €1.49     | €1.19 | €15.32       | 313        | 104 % (impossible) |
| €20         | €1.65     | €1.27 | €17.08       | 281        | 94 %               |
| €22         | €1.82     | €1.35 | €18.83       | 255        | 85 %               |
| €24         | €1.98     | €1.43 | €20.59       | 233        | 78 %               |
| €26         | €2.15     | €1.50 | €22.35       | 215        | 72 %               |
| €28         | €2.31     | €1.58 | €24.11       | 199        | 66 %               |

### Profit or loss by price and attendance

The break-even alone hides your risk. This grid shows the result for each combination of price and tickets sold (worked example, rounded, organiser absorbs the fee):

| Price \ tickets sold | 150     | 200     | 250   | 300     |
| -------------------- | ------- | ------- | ----- | ------- |
| €18                  | −€2,487 | −€1,721 | −€955 | −€189   |
| €20                  | −€2,223 | −€1,369 | −€515 | +€339   |
| €22                  | −€1,961 | −€1,019 | −€78  | +€864   |
| €24                  | −€1,696 | −€667   | +€362 | +€1,392 |
| €26                  | −€1,432 | −€315   | +€802 | +€1,920 |

How to read it:

- **Each row is your downside.** At €22 you lose about €1,000 if only 200 people come; at €26 it is about €300.
- **Each column is your pricing range.** At a realistic 250 sales, the result swings from −€955 to +€802 depending on price.
- **A higher price can mean fewer buyers.** Check the cell you actually expect, not the best one. If your realistic cell is still red, no pricing trick helps: cut fixed costs, negotiate a door deal or find a sponsor.

Then sanity-check against the market: what do comparable shows at similar venues in your city charge? Start from your costs, then adjust to the market, never the other way round.

## Should you pass the booking fee on or absorb it?

Whoever pays it, the ticketing fee is a variable cost. With a 3.9 % + €0.49 fee, the effect on our €22 ticket looks like this (worked example, as of November 2026):

|                         | Organiser absorbs the fee | Fee passed on to the buyer |
| ----------------------- | ------------------------- | -------------------------- |
| Buyer pays              | €22.00                    | €23.35                     |
| Contribution per ticket | €18.83                    | €20.18                     |
| Break-even              | 255 tickets               | 238 tickets                |
| Price optics            | round number              | uneven total               |

A popular middle way is to absorb the fee and round the price up. At €24 with the fee absorbed, break-even is 233 tickets and the poster shows a clean "€24".

Whichever route you take, EU consumer law requires that the advertised price includes all unavoidable fees. Under the Unfair Commercial Practices Directive, the price inclusive of taxes is material information in any invitation to purchase, and fees that appear only at checkout ("drip pricing") are a misleading omission. More in [dynamic ticket pricing and EU rules](/en/guides/dynamic-pricing-tickets-eu).

Because part of the fee is fixed (€0.49), it weighs more on cheap tickets: €0.88 or 8.8 % on a €10 ticket, €1.27 or 6.3 % on €20, and €2.44 or 4.9 % on €50. Our [ticketing platform fee comparison](/en/guides/ticketing-platforms-fees-compared) runs the same maths for other providers.

## How does VAT change your ticket price across Europe?

VAT on admission is due where the event physically takes place, at that country's rate. The same €22 gross price therefore leaves very different amounts behind. Rates below come from the European Commission's TEDB database for cultural events such as concerts and theatre (as of November 2026). Worked example with a 3.9 % + €0.49 fee absorbed.

| Country                                      | Rate for concerts and shows | VAT in a €22 ticket | Left after VAT and fee |
| -------------------------------------------- | --------------------------- | ------------------- | ---------------------- |
| France                                       | 5.5 %                       | €1.15               | €19.50                 |
| Belgium                                      | 6 %                         | €1.25               | €19.40                 |
| Germany (concert)                            | 7 %                         | €1.44               | €19.21                 |
| Poland                                       | 8 %                         | €1.63               | €19.02                 |
| Netherlands                                  | 9 %                         | €1.82               | €18.83                 |
| Italy, Spain                                 | 10 %                        | €2.00               | €18.65                 |

The gap between a German concert and a German club night is €2.07 per ticket at the same price. Small businesses under their national VAT threshold, and some non-profit bodies, may not charge VAT at all. ## How do you set price tiers?

**Tiered pricing** means selling the same event at several predefined prices, each with its own allocation. The classic three-step ladder:

1. **Early bird:** a limited allocation for early buyers, cheaper than standard.
2. **Standard:** your calculated main price and largest allocation.
3. **Door:** a higher price for walk-ups, while capacity lasts.

### Worked example: tiers for the Utrecht concert

| Tier       | Allocation | Price           | Contribution per ticket | Total         |
| ---------- | ---------- | --------------- | ----------------------- | ------------- |
| Early bird | 60         | €18             | €15.32                  | €919.20       |
| Standard   | 180        | €22             | €18.83                  | €3,389.40     |
| Door       | 60         | €26             | €22.35                  | €1,341.00     |
| **Total**  | **300**    | **average €22** |                         | **€5,649.60** |

At a sell-out, profit is €864.60, practically the same as a flat €22 price (€864). Tiers do not add money by themselves. Their value is timing: early-bird sales arrive weeks earlier, show you whether the event is working, and give people a reason to buy now. If you sell only 60 / 150 / 20, the result is a loss of about €594. Price the standard tier to cover costs, and treat door income as upside.

For sizing and wording, see [early bird tickets](/en/guides/early-bird-tickets). Concession, VIP or group tiers follow the same pattern: own price, own allocation, own contribution. If you sell at the door, read [box office and door sales](/en/guides/box-office-door-sales).

## Checklist: price your tickets in 10 steps

- [ ] List every fixed cost from real quotes: venue, artists, production, staff, marketing, insurance
- [ ] Check your music licensing tariff and whether it is flat or a percentage
- [ ] Confirm the VAT rate for your event type in the country where it takes place
- [ ] Add a 10 % contingency
- [ ] Calculate the ticketing fee per ticket and decide who pays it
- [ ] Work out the contribution and break-even for three to five prices
- [ ] Check that break-even sits at or below 75–80 % of capacity
- [ ] Compare prices of similar events in your area
- [ ] Set tiers with allocations that never exceed total capacity
- [ ] Publish every price as a total price including all mandatory fees

## Frequently asked questions

### How do I work out the price of an event ticket?

Add up all fixed costs plus a 10 % buffer. Decide how many tickets you will realistically sell, for example 75 % of capacity, and divide the fixed costs by that number. Then add VAT, the ticketing fee and other per-ticket costs on top. The result is your minimum price, which you then compare with the local market.

### What is break-even for an event?

Break-even is the number of tickets at which income covers all costs. You calculate it as fixed costs divided by the contribution per ticket, which is the price minus VAT, fees and other per-ticket costs. Every ticket above it is profit, every ticket below it a loss.

### How much more should door tickets cost?

There is no fixed rule. Many organisers add a few euros to the standard advance price so that buying early pays off, without turning walk-ups away. Announce the door price when sales open, and never make the door cheaper than advance tickets.

### Should I pass the ticketing fee on to buyers?

It lowers your break-even, in our example from 255 to 238 tickets, but buyers see an uneven total such as €23.35. Many organisers absorb the fee and round the price up instead. Both are legal as long as the advertised price already includes every mandatory fee.

### Does VAT really make a difference to ticket pricing?

Yes. On a €22 ticket the VAT ranges from €1.15 in France (5.5 %) to €2.62 in Ireland (13.5 %), and a German club night at 19 % carries €3.51. The rate depends on the country where the event takes place and on whether it counts as a concert or show.
