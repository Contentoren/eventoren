A charity event is an event whose surplus goes to a good cause: a benefit concert, a gala dinner, a sponsored run or a quiz night. The most important rule up front: **a ticket is not a donation.** In most European countries, money paid for admission buys something in return, so it doesn't qualify for tax relief as a gift, even if the price is "generous". Only a separate, voluntary payment on top of the ticket can count as a donation, and only a registered charity or equivalent body can confirm it. This guide shows how to separate tickets from donations, how fees affect what reaches the cause, and how to report the result honestly, as of November 2026.

> **Key takeaways**
>
> - Admission is payment for a right to attend, not a gift. Germany and the UK both reject splitting a ticket into "value plus donation".
> - A donation needs to be voluntary and clearly separate: guests must be able to attend by paying only the ticket price.
> - Say before the event who receives the money and how much per ticket; publish the result afterwards.
> - Fees reduce the surplus. In our example with 250 tickets at €30, the fee is €415 in total, either paid by the charity or by buyers.
> - Charity doesn't mean licence-free: music still has to be licensed, though some collecting societies offer conditional discounts.

This guide is not legal or tax advice.

## Which charity event formats work best?

**A charity event is legally an ordinary event.** The same rules on permits, safety, music licensing and VAT apply. "Charity" only describes where the money goes.

| Format                       | Income                                               | Effort                           | Watch out for                                                             |
| ---------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Benefit concert              | Tickets, donation box, bar                           | Artists, sound, music licence    | Artists playing for free is common, but get it in writing                 |
| Gala dinner                  | Tickets with dinner, auction, raffle, table sponsors | High: venue, catering, programme | Auction purchases and sponsorship are not donations                       |
| Sponsored run or walk        | Entry fee, pledges per lap or kilometre              | Route, first aid, lap counting   | Entry fee is a payment; pledges paid directly to the charity can be gifts |
| Fundraising party            | Tickets, drinks                                      | Medium, youth protection         | Standard VAT rates apply                                                  |
| Quiz night, talk, comedy     | Tickets, donations                                   | Low to medium                    | Small rooms often have high donation rates                                |
| Bake sale, fair, school fête | Sales, pitch fees, donation box                      | Low                              | Often no ticket needed                                                    |

Pick the format your network supports: musicians suggest a concert, business contacts suggest a gala, schools and sports clubs lean towards a sponsored run because it has almost no fixed costs.

## Is a charity ticket a donation?

No. That is the rule in the two countries where we checked the official guidance, and it's the safe assumption elsewhere until your adviser says otherwise.

### The UK: HMRC's Gift Aid rules

HMRC's guidance for charities (chapter 3.43) says a payment for a ticket or admission to a charity event "is not a gift to charity but the purchase of a right to attend". A "minimum donation" or "specified donation" required to attend is treated as the admission charge. Neither qualifies for Gift Aid.

A charity can charge a set ticket price and **ask for a suggested donation** on top, which can be gift aided, if:

- the donation is not compulsory, so guests can attend by paying only the ticket price;
- the ticket or event literature makes that clear;
- donors get no preferential treatment over non-donors, so tickets go on a first come, first served basis.

HMRC's own example: a dinner ticket at £80 plus a suggested £20 donation, clearly marked as optional. The £80 is not gift-aidable, but the £20 (or any amount above £80) can be. A £100 ticket where the charity hopes to make £40 profit per guest is just a ticket: the £40 is profit, not a donation.

### Germany: no splitting of a single payment

German income tax guidance (H 10b.1 EStH, "Gegenleistung") rules out splitting one payment into an appropriate price plus a donated remainder. A "solidarity ticket" at €50 instead of €20 for the same seat is entirely payment. A donation receipt (Zuwendungsbestätigung) is only possible for an additional, voluntary, separate payment, and only a tax-privileged body (a non-profit association, foundation or public body) may issue one. A false receipt creates liability of 30 % of the amount (§ 10b(4) EStG).

### What works in practice

| Model                                                               | Donation?                                 | Tax receipt possible?                         |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------- |
| Ticket €20, "proceeds go to the food bank"                          | No, it's payment                          | No                                            |
| "Solidarity ticket" €50 instead of €20, same seat                   | No, all payment                           | No                                            |
| Ticket €20 plus optional transfer to the charity's donation account | Ticket no, transfer yes                   | Yes, for the transfer                         |
| Free entry, donation box at the exit                                | Yes, if everyone can enter without paying | Only for named donations, not anonymous coins |
| "Donation instead of admission", but only payers get in             | No, it's an admission charge              | No                                            |
| Item won at the gala auction                                        | No, it's a purchase                       | No                                            |

**Practical tip:** collect donations through the charity's own donation page, account or box, not through the ticket shop. That keeps ticket and gift separate, and donations don't attract ticket fees.

Rules for who can issue receipts and claim relief differ across Europe (France, the Netherlands, Ireland and others each have their own regime). Check the national rules of the receiving organisation.

## Who organises: the charity, a partner or a private person?

Decide this before you print a poster, because it determines who sells tickets and who can confirm donations.

| Set-up                                                      | Who sells tickets                  | Who can confirm donations                       | Watch out for                            |
| ----------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------- |
| Registered charity or non-profit runs the event             | The charity                        | The charity                                     | Up-to-date registration or tax exemption |
| Non-profit runs it for another cause                        | The organiser                      | The organiser for gifts to it                   | Rules on passing funds to other bodies   |
| Band, company or private person organises, charity receives | The organiser                      | Only the charity, for gifts paid directly to it | Route donations to the charity's account |
| School class, informal committee                            | An adult or a parents' association | Nobody, unless a registered body takes over     | Informal groups are not charities        |

If you organise privately or as a company, ticket revenue is first your own income, and passing the surplus on is your own donation. Ask your tax adviser how that is treated in your country.

## How much reaches the cause? A worked example with fees

Guests want to know how much of their ticket helps. The answer depends a lot on who pays the ticketing fee.

**Example (as of November 2026):** a charity concert, 250 tickets at €30. The band plays for free. The organiser charges no VAT (for example, it uses a small-business exemption); if you do charge VAT, deduct it from revenue first. Ticketing with Eventoren: 3.9 % + €0.49 per paid ticket = **€1.66** per €30 ticket.

**Costs (example figures):**

| Cost item                       | Amount     |
| ------------------------------- | ---------- |
| Venue hire                      | €600       |
| Sound and light                 | €500       |
| Event liability insurance       | €90        |
| Printing and promotion          | €150       |
| Music licence                   | €200       |
| **Total excluding ticket fees** | **€1,540** |

**Option A vs option B:**

|                           | A: organiser absorbs the fee | B: buyers pay the fee |
| ------------------------- | ---------------------------- | --------------------- |
| Guest pays per ticket     | €30.00                       | €31.66                |
| Ticket fees (250 × €1.66) | €415                         | €415 (paid by buyers) |
| Payout to organiser       | €7,085                       | €7,500                |
| minus costs               | €1,540                       | €1,540                |
| **Surplus for the cause** | **€5,545**                   | **€5,960**            |
| Per ticket to the cause   | €22.18                       | €23.84                |

Option B brings €415 more to the cause, but guests see an uneven final price. A middle way: set the price at **€32** and absorb the fee. The fee is then €1.74 per ticket, you keep €30.26, and guests see a round number. For more on pricing, see [how to price event tickets](/en/guides/how-to-price-event-tickets).

## How do you show transparently what reaches the cause?

Vague promises ("proceeds go to…") cause questions afterwards. Good charity communication states the rule beforehand and the number afterwards.

**Before, in the shop and on posters:**

- Name the recipient in full, not just "children in need".
- Say exactly what is passed on: "the entire surplus after costs" or "€15 from every ticket". A fixed amount per ticket is the easiest for guests to understand.
- Optionally share the cost structure: "of €30, about €22 is expected to reach…".
- Separate clearly: "Your ticket is not a donation. If you'd like to give more, you can donate directly to [charity]."
- Never write "tax-deductible" next to the ticket price.

**Afterwards, within a few weeks:**

- Publish the surplus with an income and expenditure summary.
- Show the transfer receipt or a thank-you letter from the recipient.
- Thank sponsors and helpers who covered or reduced costs.

## Do charity events need a music licence?

Yes. **Charity doesn't mean licence-free.** Public performance of music needs a licence from your national collecting society, whether the money goes to charity or not. Each country has its own society and tariffs, so check before you announce the event.

Some societies offer discounts for charitable events, usually with strict conditions. **Germany's GEMA**, for example, grants a 10 % benefit discount only if all of these are met:

- [ ] The entire net proceeds go exclusively to people in need.
- [ ] All performers confirm in writing that they waive their fee entirely.
- [ ] You provide a transfer receipt showing how much went to which organisation.
- [ ] You provide a detailed income and expenditure statement.
- [ ] The event was registered as a benefit event beforehand, and proof is sent on time (six weeks for the U-V tariff).

A concert for a new school stage doesn't count as helping people in need, and even a small fee for the band loses the discount. Plan the licence as a cost line from day one.

## Checklist: organise a charity event

**8–12 weeks before**

- [ ] Cause and recipient fixed; recipient's charity registration checked
- [ ] Organiser clarified: charity, partner or private person
- [ ] Hand-over agreed: whole surplus or fixed amount per ticket
- [ ] Performers' fee waivers in writing
- [ ] Budget with costs, VAT, music licence and ticket fees
- [ ] Decided who pays the ticket fee

**4–8 weeks before**

- [ ] Music licence applied for, as a benefit event if a discount exists
- [ ] Permits and bar licence sorted with the local authority
- [ ] Ticket shop set up with recipient and hand-over rule in the text
- [ ] Separate donation route set up (charity account, box, the charity's own form)
- [ ] Raffle or lottery rules checked with the authority

**On the day and after**

- [ ] Donation box sealed, counted by two people with a written record
- [ ] Income and expenditure statement prepared
- [ ] Surplus transferred, receipt filed
- [ ] Licence paperwork submitted on time
- [ ] Tax receipts issued only for genuine donations, by the eligible body
- [ ] Result published, guests, sponsors and helpers thanked

## Frequently asked questions

### Is a charity event ticket tax-deductible?

Generally no. A ticket buys the right to attend, so it isn't a gift. HMRC's Gift Aid guidance and German tax guidance both say so. Only a separate, voluntary donation on top of the ticket can qualify.

### Can I call part of the ticket price a donation?

Not if guests have to pay it to attend. HMRC treats a "minimum donation" as part of the admission charge, and German guidance rules out splitting a single payment. Offer an optional donation that guests can skip and still attend.

### Can a private person organise a charity event?

Yes, but a private person can't issue donation receipts. Ticket revenue is first your own income. Route donations directly to the charity's account so it can confirm them, and document how you pass on the surplus.

### Do I need a music licence for a charity concert?

Yes. Charity events are public performances like any other. Some collecting societies offer discounts with strict conditions; Germany's GEMA, for example, gives 10 % only if all performers waive their fees and the whole surplus helps people in need.

### Should buyers or the charity pay the ticket fee?

If buyers pay, more reaches the cause: €415 more in our example of 250 tickets at €30. If the organiser pays, guests see a round price. Raising the price slightly and absorbing the fee combines both.
