Since **28 June 2025**, online ticket shops that sell to consumers in the EU fall under the **European Accessibility Act** (Directive (EU) 2019/882) as "e-commerce services". They must be perceivable, operable, understandable and robust, including the payment step, and the provider must publish information on how the service meets the requirements. **Microenterprises providing services** (fewer than 10 staff and no more than €2 million turnover or balance sheet) are exempt. In practice, the benchmark is the harmonised standard EN 301 549, which points to WCAG 2.1 level AA. If you sell through a ticketing platform, the shop duties mainly sit with the platform in our reading. Your event pages, your own website and the venue are still on you.

> **Key takeaways**
>
> - The EAA covers services provided to consumers after 28 June 2025, including e-commerce. Online ticket sales to consumers fit that definition. Pure B2B sales do not.
> - Microenterprises providing services are exempt: fewer than 10 people employed and annual turnover or balance sheet total of at most €2 million.
> - Conformity with harmonised standards gives a presumption of conformity. For websites, that is EN 301 549, which references WCAG 2.1.
> - Each country enforces through national law and authorities. Germany allows fines up to €100,000; France up to €50,000 (as of November 2026).
> - Accessibility doesn't stop at checkout: step-free access, companion tickets, clear information and briefed staff matter just as much.

This guide is not legal or tax advice.

## What is the European Accessibility Act?

**The European Accessibility Act (EAA) is an EU directive that sets common accessibility requirements for selected products and services, so that people with disabilities can use them on an equal basis.** It was adopted in 2019. Member States had to transpose it by June 2022 and apply the national rules from 28 June 2025 (Art. 31).

The EAA covers products such as computers, smartphones and ticketing machines, and services such as banking, e-books, passenger transport and **e-commerce**. It defines e-commerce services as "services provided at a distance, through websites and mobile device-based services by electronic means and at the individual request of a consumer with a view to concluding a consumer contract" (Art. 3(30)). The recitals add that these obligations apply "to the online sale of any product or service". An online ticket shop for concerts, parties or festivals fits squarely.

| EAA at a glance  | Content                                                                                           |
| ---------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Legal act        | Directive (EU) 2019/882, transposed into national law                                             |
| Applies from     | 28 June 2025, for services provided to consumers after that date (Art. 2(2))                      |
| Relevant service | E-commerce services (Art. 3(30))                                                                  |
| Requirements     | Annex I, Sections III and IV(g)                                                                   |
| Information duty | Annex V, via terms or an equivalent document                                                      |
| Exemption        | Microenterprises providing services (Art. 4(5))                                                   |
| Standard         | Harmonised standards give a presumption of conformity (Art. 15); in practice EN 301 549           |
| Transition       | Service contracts agreed before 28 June 2025 may run until they expire, max. five years (Art. 32) |

### The microenterprise exemption

Art. 4(5) says "microenterprises providing services shall be exempt from complying with the accessibility requirements" for services and related obligations. A **microenterprise** "employs fewer than 10 persons and … has an annual turnover not exceeding EUR 2 million or an annual balance sheet total not exceeding EUR 2 million" (Art. 3(23)). The staff condition and one of the financial conditions must both be met.

**Examples (our assessment, as of November 2026):**

- A volunteer-run sports club with one part-time employee and €60,000 turnover: microenterprise, exempt.
- A club venue with 25 casual staff and €900,000 turnover: not a microenterprise, because it has 10 or more staff.
- A concert promoter with 6 employees and €3.5 million ticket revenue: not a microenterprise, unless its balance sheet total is €2 million or less.

How part-time and casual staff are counted, and how connected companies are grouped, depends on national guidance. Germany's labour ministry has published guidelines for microenterprises, as the directive requires Member States to do (Art. 4(6)). In borderline cases, ask an adviser.

### Who is responsible on a ticketing platform?

The EAA places duties on the **service provider** (Art. 13). If a platform operates the shop, basket, checkout and payment, it is the one providing the e-commerce service, and in our reading it carries the requirements for those parts. No court or authority has ruled specifically on ticketing platforms yet. Typically, responsibilities split like this:

| Area                                                | Responsible (assessment)                      | What you can do                                          |
| --------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------- |
| Checkout, basket, payment                           | Platform                                      | Ask for its accessibility information                    |
| Event texts, images, ticket descriptions you upload | You                                           | Plain language, alt text, no key info only inside images |
| Your own website leading to the shop                | You, if not exempt and purchase happens there | Accessible navigation to the ticket link                 |
| Embedded widget on your site                        | Shared                                        | Don't override contrast or focus styles                  |
| PDFs, venue maps, directions                        | You                                           | Provide as text, not just images                         |
| Venue, entrance, staff                              | You (not EAA, but good hosting)               | See the section on accessible events                     |

## What does an accessible ticket shop need?

Annex I, Section III requires services to provide information through more than one sensory channel, in text that assistive tools can use, with adequate font size, contrast and spacing, and to make websites and apps "perceivable, operable, understandable and robust". For e-commerce, Section IV(g) adds that **identification, security and payment functions** must meet the same four principles, and that accessibility information about the products sold must be passed on where the responsible operator supplies it.

### EN 301 549 and WCAG 2.1 AA

Art. 15 gives a **presumption of conformity** to services that comply with harmonised standards published in the Official Journal. For digital services, the standard the market works with is **EN 301 549**. Its web chapter maps to the success criteria of **WCAG 2.1**, and audits usually test against level AA. Germany's federal accessibility agency names EN 301 549 V3.2.1 as the central reference and notes that a revision based on WCAG 2.2 is in progress. Check the current status before commissioning an audit, and write the version into the contract.

| Check       | Typical ticket shop failure                           | Fix                                                                  |
| ----------- | ----------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Contrast    | Pale grey prices, white text on a brand-colour button | At least 4.5:1 for normal text, 3:1 for large text (WCAG 2.1, 1.4.3) |
| Keyboard    | Quantity only changeable with a mouse                 | Every control reachable by Tab, visible focus                        |
| Forms       | No labels, errors shown only in red                   | Visible labels, error text explaining how to fix it                  |
| Images      | Flyer as the only source of date and venue            | Date, venue, price as text; alt text                                 |
| Time limits | Basket expires silently                               | Warning and a way to extend                                          |
| Language    | Jargon and abbreviations                              | Plain wording: "price includes booking fee"                          |
| CAPTCHA     | Visual puzzle only                                    | An alternative that doesn't depend on sight                          |
| Payment     | Payment pop-up unusable with a screen reader          | Accessible payment methods, focus stays in the dialog                |
| Ticket PDF  | QR code only                                          | Event name, date, venue and ticket type as real text                 |

### Disproportionate burden

Art. 14 lets a provider apply requirements only as far as they don't impose a **disproportionate burden** or fundamentally alter the service. You must document that assessment using the criteria in Annex VI, keep it for five years and renew it at least every five years. The recitals are clear that "lack of priority, time or knowledge" is not a legitimate reason. Treat this as a narrow exception, not a way out.

## What goes into an accessibility statement?

Art. 13(2) and **Annex V** require the provider to explain how the service meets the requirements, in the terms and conditions "or equivalent document", available to the public in an accessible way. The information should contain:

- [ ] a general description of the service in accessible formats
- [ ] descriptions needed to understand how the service operates
- [ ] a description of how the relevant requirements of Annex I are met
- [ ] where national law requires it, the competent authority (Germany, for example, asks for the market surveillance authority)

**Example structure** (adapt it to your situation):

> **Accessibility of our ticket shop**
>
> You can buy tickets for our events in this shop. Buying consists of choosing tickets, entering contact details, paying and receiving your ticket by email.
>
> We follow EN 301 549 and WCAG 2.1 level AA. All functions work with a keyboard, text has sufficient contrast, form fields are labelled and errors are shown as text. Known limitations: [list them honestly].
>
> Report a barrier: [email, phone]. Competent authority: [national authority].

If you use a platform, ask for its accessibility information and link to it from your own website.

## How is the EAA enforced across Europe?

Each Member State sets penalties (Art. 30) and names authorities. Selected examples, as of November 2026:

| Country     | National law                                                                                | Enforcement                                                                                                                             |
| ----------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Germany     | Barrierefreiheitsstärkungsgesetz (BFSG), § 3(3) exempts microenterprises providing services | Joint market surveillance body of the federal states (MLBF) in Magdeburg, active since September 2025; fines up to €100,000 (§ 37 BFSG) |
| France      | Loi n° 2023-171, art. 16, and Décret n° 2023-931                                            | Fines up to €50,000 (secondary compilation)                                                                                             |
| Netherlands | Implementation act (Stb. 2024, 87); e-commerce rules in the Civil Code (art. 6:230fa)       | Authority for Consumers and Markets (ACM)                                                                                               |
| Ireland     | S.I. No. 636/2023, covering "e-commerce services"                                           | Competition and Consumer Protection Commission (CCPC)                                                                                   |

In Germany, almost 700 reports reached the MLBF by 1 June 2026. We know of no fines against ticket shops so far; an order to fix barriers is the likelier first step.

Selling cross-border? A Belgian or Austrian buyer brings that country's transposition into play, and providers outside the EU, including UK businesses, are not automatically out of scope.

## How do you make the event itself accessible?

The EAA only covers the digital sale. Whether disabled guests can actually attend is decided at the venue. The best practical lists come from national accessibility agencies and disability organisations: accessible booking and information, getting there, step-free routes and accessible toilets, support such as sign language interpretation or hearing loops, and trained staff.

### Companion tickets

Many people need a companion or personal assistant to attend at all. Rules on free companion entry vary. In Germany, for example, the "B" mark in a disability ID card shows that the holder is entitled to bring a companion, but there's no general legal duty for private organisers to admit them free. Many organisers offer a free companion ticket anyway, because the companion is a precondition for attending, not a choice.

- Create a separate ticket type "Companion" at €0 with its own capacity.
- Ask to see proof at the door; don't store copies.
- Sell wheelchair spaces as their own ticket type with a small capacity.

### Information before the event

Write into every event description:

- step-free access yes or no, door widths, lift
- accessible toilet and where it is
- seating at standing gigs
- volume, lighting effects, strobe
- a named contact for access questions, with email

## Checklist: accessible ticket shop and event

**Legal**

- [ ] Check whether you are a microenterprise (under 10 staff and at most €2 million turnover or balance sheet).
- [ ] Clarify who runs the shop: you or a platform.
- [ ] Request the platform's accessibility information and link to it.
- [ ] Note the national law and authority for each country you sell into.

**Shop and website**

- [ ] Check colours for contrast (4.5:1 for normal text).
- [ ] Complete a whole purchase with the keyboard only.
- [ ] Test once with a screen reader (NVDA on Windows, VoiceOver on iPhone or Mac).
- [ ] Put all event information in text, add alt text to images.
- [ ] Publish an accessibility page with the Annex V content.
- [ ] Name a contact for barrier reports and answer within days.

**Event**

- [ ] Check the venue for step-free access, door widths and an accessible toilet.
- [ ] Set up companion and wheelchair ticket types.
- [ ] Add access, toilet, volume and lighting information to the event page.
- [ ] Brief door staff on companions, assistance dogs and alternative entrances.
- [ ] For talks and conferences, ask early about sign language or captions.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does the European Accessibility Act apply to online ticket sales?

Yes, if consumers can buy tickets through the website or app and you are not an exempt microenterprise. Online ticket sales are an e-commerce service under Art. 3(30) of Directive (EU) 2019/882, and the rules apply to services provided after 28 June 2025.

### Are small clubs and organisers exempt from the EAA?

Usually yes. Microenterprises providing services, meaning fewer than 10 staff and at most €2 million annual turnover or balance sheet total, are exempt (Art. 4(5)). That covers most clubs, charities and small promoters.

### Who is responsible if I sell through a ticketing platform?

In our reading, the platform is responsible for the shop and checkout it operates as service provider. You remain responsible for your own content, images and website. No authority or court has yet ruled specifically on ticketing platforms.

### Which standard applies to an accessible ticket shop?

Harmonised standards give a presumption of conformity under Art. 15. In practice that means EN 301 549, which references WCAG 2.1 for web content, and audits usually test level AA.

### Do companions have to get in free?

There is no EU-wide rule, and national rules differ. Many organisers offer a free companion ticket voluntarily because the companion is what makes attendance possible.
