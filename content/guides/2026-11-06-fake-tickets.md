You can rarely tell a fake ticket from the ticket itself. You spot it from the offer around it: a price that doesn't fit the demand, a seller who rushes you, and a payment method without buyer protection. A QR code proves nothing, because only the organiser's system can tell whether it is valid and unused, and that check happens at the door. The safest way to buy is the official ticket shop linked from the organiser's or venue's website, or the organiser's own resale. If you've already been scammed, contact your bank or payment provider immediately, keep the evidence and report it to the police.

> **Key takeaways**
>
> - Buy from the official seller named on the organiser's, venue's or artist's website. Don't trust the first sponsored search result.
> - Red flags: sold-out event at face value or below, pressure to pay now, bank transfer or "friends and family" payment, gift cards, crypto.
> - A QR code cannot be verified by eye or with a QR app. The same PDF or screenshot can be sold many times, and only the first scan gets in.
> - If scammed: call your bank or card issuer at once, save all evidence, report to the police and to the platform where you found the offer.
> - SEPA direct debits can be reversed within 8 weeks, and unauthorised payments reported up to 13 months later. Card disputes follow the card scheme's rules.

## What is a fake ticket?

**A fake ticket is any ticket sold as valid entry that will not get you in.** That covers more than forged PDFs. As of November 2026, the common types are:

| Type                            | What it looks like                               | Why it fails                            |
| ------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------- |
| Forged ticket                   | Home-made PDF with an invented code              | Code is unknown to the ticketing system |
| Ticket sold several times       | Genuine PDF or screenshot sold to several buyers | Only the first scan is valid            |
| Ticket in someone else's name   | Real personalised ticket, wrong name             | Name or ID check at the door            |
| Ticket from a fake shop         | Website copies the official shop                 | No contract with the organiser          |
| Ticket for a non-existent event | Offer for a show that was never announced        | The event doesn't happen                |
| Ticket never delivered          | Seller takes the money and disappears            | No ticket exists                        |

The UK's national fraud reporting service describes ticket fraud the same way: you buy tickets for a concert, festival, sports event or comedy show, but they never arrive or turn out to be fake, and you aren't refunded.

## What are the warning signs of a ticket scam?

The more of these signals you see together, the more careful you should be.

### The price doesn't fit the situation

If an event sold out within minutes, tickets at face value or below are unusual. A very high price isn't proof of authenticity either: scammers charge markups too.

### Payment without buyer protection

This is the most important signal. Walk away if the seller insists on:

- a bank transfer or instant transfer to a private person
- PayPal "friends and family" instead of "goods and services"
- gift cards, prepaid vouchers or money transfer services
- cryptocurrency
- cash sent by post

Citizens Information in Ireland warns that a seller asking for a bank transfer could be running a scam. The UK fraud reporting service advises avoiding bank transfers to people you don't know, because credit cards or payment services like PayPal give you a better chance of getting your money back.

### Pressure and urgency

"I've got three other people interested, you need to pay now." Pressure stops you from checking. Take your time: a genuine seller will wait an hour.

### New or odd seller profiles

- Account created recently, no or few reviews
- Many tickets for different sold-out events at once
- Vague answers about where and when the ticket was bought
- Wants to move the chat off the platform quickly
- Sends a photo of an ID as "proof". It may be stolen and proves nothing

### Comments and direct messages

Replies like "I have spare tickets, DM me" under an artist's or venue's posts are a classic route. Accounts that imitate official profiles are a red flag. Many organisers never sell by direct message.

### Fake shops that copy the real one

Fake shops copy text, images and layout. Watch for a web address that is almost right (extra words, hyphens, a different ending), payment only by bank transfer, missing company details, and links from ads or social media rather than from the organiser's website.

## How do you check a seller before you pay?

Ask these questions before buying anywhere other than the official shop.

| Question                         | Good sign                                                                                      | Warning sign                                        |
| -------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------- |
| Where did you buy the ticket?    | Names the shop, date and price; can show the order confirmation with personal data blacked out | Evasive, can't remember                             |
| Is the ticket personalised?      | Offers an official name change via the organiser                                               | Says names are "never checked"                      |
| How do I pay?                    | Platform escrow, card or payment service with buyer protection, payment on handover            | Bank transfer only, gift card, "friends and family" |
| How is the ticket handed over?   | Official resale or name change, or in person                                                   | PDF by email, immediately                           |
| What does the profile look like? | Older account, genuine reviews                                                                 | New, no reviews, lots of tickets                    |
| How does the conversation go?    | Patient, specific answers                                                                      | Pushes, moves to a messenger app                    |

Search for the shop's or seller's name together with words like "scam" or "reviews". In the UK, the fraud reporting service also suggests checking whether a ticket seller is a member of the Society of Ticket Agents and Retailers (STAR), which runs an approved dispute resolution scheme.

## Can you check whether a QR code ticket is real?

**Not by looking at it, and not with a QR scanner app on your phone.**

A QR code is just an encoded string of characters. Whether it belongs to a valid, paid and unused ticket is only known to the ticketing system, and that check happens when the code is scanned at the entrance. That has three consequences:

- **A genuine code can still be worthless.** If one PDF was sold to five people, it is "real" for all five. Only the first person scanned gets in.
- **A professional-looking ticket proves nothing.** Logos, serial numbers and layout can be copied in minutes.
- **A QR app test doesn't help.** It shows you the content of the code, not whether the system accepts it.

What actually protects you:

1. **The organiser's official resale.** If the old ticket is cancelled and a new one issued in your name, multiple sales are impossible.
2. **An official name change** for personalised tickets. Pay only after the organiser confirms the change.
3. **Buying from someone you know**, who received the ticket directly from the official shop.

Our guide to [ticket resale rules in Europe](/en/guides/ticket-resale-rules-europe) explains when private resale is allowed at all and why names on tickets matter.

## Where is it safest to buy tickets?

Your choice of source matters more than any check you can do.

| Source                                                              | Risk          | If things go wrong                                                  |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Official ticket shop linked from the organiser's or venue's website | low           | Claims against the organiser, e.g. refund if the event is cancelled |
| Organiser's official resale                                         | low           | Ticket is usually reissued in your name                             |
| Friends, family, colleagues                                         | low to medium | Sorted out personally                                               |
| Secondary marketplace                                               | medium        | Seller is usually unknown; refunds harder                           |
| Classifieds, social media, group chats                              | high          | Claims only against the seller, if they can be traced               |

Citizens Advice in England points out that you're unlikely to get a refund if you bought from a reselling website, a private seller or a fan-to-fan site. If the event is cancelled, refunds go to the original buyer. Our guide on [event cancellation refunds in the EU](/en/guides/event-cancellation-refunds-eu) explains your rights when you bought from the official seller.

To find the official shop, go to the organiser's, venue's or artist's website and follow the ticket link from there. Check the address bar before you enter card details.

## What should you do if you've bought fake tickets?

Act fast. With some payment methods, time decides whether you get your money back.

### Step 1: Stop or reverse the payment

- **Bank transfer:** call your bank immediately and ask whether the transfer can be recalled. Once the money has left, it is often hard to get back.
- **SEPA direct debit:** under the EU Payment Services Directive (PSD2, Art. 76 and 77), you can ask your bank to refund an authorised direct debit within 8 weeks, without giving a reason.
- **Unauthorised payment** (someone misused your details): report it to your bank without delay, and at the latest within 13 months of the debit (PSD2 Art. 71).
- **Credit or debit card:** ask your card issuer about a chargeback. The card schemes (Visa, Mastercard and others) set the reasons and deadlines, and your bank applies them. Deadlines exist, so don't wait.
- **PayPal or escrow:** open a dispute or buyer-protection case with the provider.
- **Money transfer service:** contact the provider straight away and ask them to stop the transfer.

### Step 2: Save the evidence

Take screenshots before the seller deletes anything: the listing or shop page, the profile, the chat, emails, the ticket PDF, the payment receipt and the seller's bank or account details.

### Step 3: Report it to the police

Report the fraud to the police in your country. Many forces have an online reporting service. In the UK, ticket fraud is reported to the national fraud reporting service (Report Fraud, formerly Action Fraud) online or on 0300 123 2040. Give the police reference to your bank and the platform; it often helps your claim.

### Step 4: Report the listing and tell the organiser

Report the listing, profile or shop to the platform where you found it. Tell the organiser if its event name is being misused, so it can warn other fans.

### Step 5: Get help with cross-border cases

If you bought from a trader based in another EU country, Iceland or Norway, the **European Consumer Centre (ECC) in your own country** can explain your rights and help settle the dispute. The ECC Network is co-funded by the European Commission, and you must contact the centre in the country where you live. In Ireland, Citizens Information also points to the Competition and Consumer Protection Commission (CCPC) and the small claims procedure.

## Frequently asked questions

### How can I tell if a ticket is fake?

Usually not from the ticket. Genuine and fake PDFs look the same, and a QR code can't be checked without the ticketing system. Focus on the offer: price, payment method, pressure and the seller's profile.

### Is a screenshot of a ticket a sign of a scam?

Not necessarily, as honest sellers send screenshots too. But a screenshot can be sold to any number of people, and only the first scan gets in. Prefer the organiser's resale or an official name change.

### Will I get my money back if I bought a fake ticket?

That depends mostly on how you paid. Card payments, direct debits and buyer-protection schemes give you a much better chance than a bank transfer. Contact your bank or payment provider immediately, report it to the police and keep every receipt.

### Is the organiser responsible if I bought a fake ticket?

Usually not. Your contract was with the seller, not with the organiser. If you bought a genuine personalised ticket in someone else's name, the organiser can refuse entry and generally doesn't owe you a refund.

### Where can I get help if the seller is in another EU country?

Contact the European Consumer Centre in the country where you live. The ECC Network helps with disputes with traders in other EU countries, Iceland and Norway. For pure fraud by a private person, report it to the police as well.
